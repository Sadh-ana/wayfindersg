Agreements for shared rooms and flats

A twenty-minute conversation on sleep, noise, guests and cleaning that prevents most of the arguments.

What this coversGeneral orientation. Your residence or tenancy may set binding rules on guests, noise or sub-letting; those rules override this guide.
Do this first · 2 to 10 minutes

Send one message to your roommate: ‘Can we spend 20 minutes this week agreeing a few basics? I will bring a list.’

Roommate friction is rarely about one big thing. It is usually a quiet series of small things nobody said out loud: lights on at midnight, a friend sleeping over, dishes in the sink. The fix is boring and works: agree the basics early, in a calm moment, before anyone is annoyed.

What to cover in the first talk

  • Sleep: usual bedtimes, whether lights and screens are OK late, alarms.
  • Noise: calls, music, headphones, study hours.
  • Guests: how much notice, overnight or not, who is asked.
  • Cleaning: who does what, how often, and what ‘clean’ means to each of you.
  • Shared things: food, supplies, who buys what, what is off-limits.
  • Bills (flats): who pays which bill, by when, and how it is split.

When something goes wrong

  1. Say it early and small, in person if possible: ‘When X happens, I find it hard to Y. Could we try Z?’
  2. Offer a trade rather than a demand: ‘I can do the bins if you keep the music down after 11.’
  3. If it keeps happening, write down the dates and what happened, then speak to your residential advisor or the housing office if you have one.
What can waitYou do not need a formal contract between roommates for a hall room. A written list you both agreed to is enough to point back to.
What varies, so check yoursResidences differ on rules, who mediates, and how room changes work. A rented flat adds a tenancy agreement; read it before you sign.

Sources

This guide makes no official-rule claims, so it cites none. It is general orientation. Where rules matter (your residence, your bank, your employer), their own pages are the source.
